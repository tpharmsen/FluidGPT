\chapter{Training and Validation}

\section{Challenges}

\subsection{Universal training framework}

As the training and validation of the foundation models is dependent on available compute resources, and the models needs to be trained in a similar way for fair comparison, a universal training framework was developed to support different datasets, models, and hardware platforms. The framework needs to support parallel training on multiple GPUs, and needs to be able to run on different hardware platforms, including local workstations and high-performance computing clusters. The framework also needs to support containerization and SLURM for easy deployment and management of training jobs.

\subsection{Compute throughput and storage space}

As the dataset size is large, and the models are complex, training requires significant compute power. The training framework needs to be able to efficiently utilize available compute resources, including multiple GPUs and distributed computing environments. The framework also needs to support mixed precision training to reduce memory usage and improve training speed. Also, the framework needs to support efficient data loading and storage management.

\subsection{Validation metrics}

To compare the performance of different models, a set of relevant validation metrics needs to be defined. The metrics need to be able to capture the accuracy of raw predictions and for the probabilistic models, the quality of the predicted distribution. The metrics need to be able to capture the accuracy of short-term predictions and long-term rollouts, as well as the spectral fidelity of the predicted flow fields. 
\section{Training Procedure}

\subsection{Training Framework and System Abstraction}

To support large-scale experimentation across heterogeneous compute environments, a custom training framework was developed to operate seamlessly on both local workstations and high-performance computing (HPC) systems. The framework specifically targets SLURM-managed and container-based clusters while retaining full compatibility with single-node and multi-GPU setups. This abstraction ensures that identical training and validation logic is reused across environments, promoting reproducibility and eliminating environment-specific code paths.

The framework is fully modular and configured through declarative configuration files, enabling model architectures, optimization hyperparameters, data handling routines, and distributed training settings to be adjusted without modifying core training code. This design was critical for systematically comparing multiple model classes under controlled and reproducible experimental conditions.

\subsection{Model Training}

Four foundation models were trained within this unified framework. Descriptions of the deterministic and probabilistic models are found in Chapter \ref{ch:models}.

For the flow-matching model, the prior distribution is constructed on-the-fly using correlated Gaussian noise. Specifically, pure Gaussian noise is filtered with a Gaussian kernel with a standard deviation of 4 in the spatial directions and 1 in the temporal direction, introducing spatial and temporal correlation. This spatial correlation of the prior serves two purposes: it reduces the sharpness of fluid interfaces in the sampled prior, and it reduces the volume of the state space from which the model must learn to transport samples to the target distribution, thereby simplifying the learning task.

\subsection{Optimization and Learning Rate Scheduling}

Both model classes were trained using the same optimization strategy to facilitate a fair comparison. Learning rates were tuned via a schedule comprising an initial linear warmup phase followed by stepwise decay at predefined milestones. The warmup phase mitigates optimization instability during early training, which is particularly pronounced for large models and distributed settings. Milestone-based decay subsequently improves convergence during the later stages of training.

Due to the stochastic sampling nature of flow-matching inference, a slightly higher initial learning rate was applied to the flow-matching models compared to the deterministic AR counterparts. Additionally, flow-matching models were trained for a longer duration at pre-decay learning rates to account for the increased complexity of learning a conditional distribution. All training was performed in \texttt{bfloat16} reduced precision to balance computational efficiency with numerical stability.

At each epoch the model is saved and the validation error is computed. The model with the lowest validation error is selected as the final model for full evaluation. This early stopping strategy ensures that the final reported results reflect the best generalization performance observed during training, rather than the final epoch which may be affected by overfitting.

\section{Hardware Platform and Parallelism}

All large-scale experiments were conducted on the revolutionary NVIDIA DGX B200 unified AI platform, which provides a high-bandwidth GPU interconnect, large per-device memory capacity, and high aggregate compute throughput. Using Docker, training and evaluation was executed within customized containerized environments to ensure consistent software dependencies across experimental runs, maximizing reproducibility and optimized device utilization.

Training was distributed across 8 GPUs using data parallelism, resulting in an effective batch size equal to eight times the per-device batch size of 176, yielding an effective batch size of 1408. This level of parallelism was essential both to stabilize the optimization dynamics and to maintain adequate throughput given the high-dimensional spatiotemporal data and large model capacity.

\section{Training hyperparameters}

All hyperparameters related to optimization, learning rate scheduling, and distributed training settings are detailed in the Table \ref{tab:fluidgpt_hparams}. Most of the hyperparameters were shared between the deterministic and probabilistic models to ensure a fair comparison. The differences in Learning rate step-down milestones is motivated by the different training durations and convergence dynamics of the two model classes. The flow-matching models were trained for a longer duration at pre-decay learning rates to allow sufficient time for learning the more complex conditional distribution, while the deterministic AR models converged more quickly and thus benefited from earlier decay. The different weight decay parameter reflects the different regularization needs of the two model classes, with the flow-matching models requiring a much smaller weight decay to avoid underfitting as the state space of the target distribution is much larger than that of the deterministic model.
Trivially, the data split seed was kept constant across all experiments to ensure that the same training and validation splits were used for both model classes, further enhancing the comparability of results.

\section{Validation}

For each dataset, 20\% of the available trajectories were reserved as a held-out validation split and were not used during training. All validation metrics were computed exclusively on this split. Validation was designed to assess three complementary aspects of model quality: short-term predictive accuracy, long-horizon temporal stability, and spectral fidelity to the underlying physics.

\subsection{Error Metrics}

The primary pointwise error metrics are the per-sample relative root mean square error (RRMSE) and the per-sample relative absolute error (RAE), defined as

\begin{equation}
	\text{RRMSE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sqrt{\frac{\left( y_i - \hat{y}_i \right)^2}{y_i^2}},
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	\text{RAE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{ \left| y_i - \hat{y}_i \right|}{\left| y_i \right|},
\end{equation}

where $y_i$ is the ground-truth value, $\hat{y}_i$ is the corresponding prediction, and $N$ is the total number of samples. These scale-invariant metrics allow meaningful comparison across datasets and model configurations. All validation was performed in \texttt{float32} precision to prioritize accurate error measurement over computational efficiency.

\subsection{Model- and Dataset-Specific Validation}

\paragraph{Deterministic Model.}
The deterministic AR model produces a single, fixed prediction for a given input sequence. Both single-step and rollout evaluations therefore yield a deterministic predicted trajectory, which is compared directly against the ground truth using pointwise and spectral metrics. Autoregressive rollout is straightforward: the predicted output timeblock is appended to the input window and fed back as the input for the subsequent prediction step.

\paragraph{Flow-Matching Model.}
The probabilistic flow-matching model defines a conditional distribution over future states. During validation, 16 independent samples were drawn for each input sequence, allowing analysis of the predicted distribution's spread and calibration. For the single next-timeblock validation on the Amira dataset, a reduced number of 8 samples were drawn due to computational constraints. Error distributions are computed in a per-sample fashion and subsequently aggregated across all drawn samples.

As the flow-matching model predicts \emph{state increments} rather than absolute states, generating the next physical state requires numerical integration in \emph{flow-time} $t_f$. Flow-time is an auxiliary continuous variable that parameterizes the learned transport map from the prior distribution to the target distribution. Formally, given a prior sample $x(0)$ and a learned velocity field $v_\theta(x, t_f)$ conditioned on the current physical state, the predicted state is obtained as

\begin{equation}
	x(1) = x(0) + \int_{0}^{1} v_\theta\bigl(x(t_f), t_f\bigr) \, \mathrm{d}{t_f}.
\end{equation}

For validation, this integral is approximated using a simple Euler scheme with 20 discrete flow-time steps. The prior distribution used at inference time is identical to that used during training. After a sample has been mapped to a physical state through the integration process, it can be fed back as conditioning input to the model in order to enable autoregressive rollouts. Note that the flow-matching model's autoregressive rollout is inherently stochastic due to the sampling of the prior at each step, resulting in a distribution of predicted trajectories rather than a single deterministic trajectory. 

\subsubsection{Single-Step Validation}

As an initial evaluation, both models were assessed in a single next-timeblock prediction setting. Given a ground-truth input sequence, each model predicts the immediately following temporal block without feeding back its own predictions. This isolates one-step transition accuracy and eliminates error accumulation effects that arise in autoregressive rollouts. The reported metric is the average per-sample error over the next 5 predicted timesteps, aggregated across all input-output pairs in the validation dataset.

\subsubsection{Rollout Validation}

To evaluate long-term temporal behavior, both models were deployed in an autoregressive rollout configuration. Starting from the first 5 ground-truth physical states of each validation trajectory, model predictions were recursively fed back as inputs to generate the full future trajectory. Prediction errors were computed at each timestep throughout the rollout horizon, enabling analysis of error accumulation and long-term stability. Note that the first timestep containing the initialization conditions of the fluid trajectory is also included and thus this evaluation step assesses the model's ability to handle non-natural initial conditions, which is a critical aspect of model robustness.

\subsubsection{Spectral Diagnostics}

In addition to pointwise metrics, validation included physics-informed spectral diagnostics to assess whether the models correctly reproduce the scale distribution of energy and enstrophy. For each predicted and ground-truth state, the log-scaled kinetic energy spectrum $E(k)$ and enstrophy spectrum $Z(k)$ were computed by Fourier-transforming the velocity field and aggregating spectral contributions over isotropic shells in wavenumber space, as described in Section \ref{sec:energyspectra}.

Scale-resolved spectral errors were defined as

\begin{equation}
	\bar{R}_E(k)
	=
	\frac{1}{N}
	\sum_{i=1}^{N}
	\frac{E_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(i)}(k)}
		{E_{\mathrm{true}}^{(i)}(k)},
	\qquad
	\bar{R}_Z(k)
	=
	\frac{1}{N}
	\sum_{i=1}^{N}
	\frac{Z_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(i)}(k)}
		{Z_{\mathrm{true}}^{(i)}(k)}.
\end{equation}

As each fluid trajectory has its own reference spectra corresponding to the system state at that time $t$, it is not meaningful to show an average graph of the spectra across the dataset. Instead, we analyze the distribution of spectral errors across the dataset by calculating the ratio between the predicted and true spectra for each trajectory, and then averaging this ratio across all trajectories to obtain a distribution of spectral errors as a function of wavenumber. This approach allows us to assess whether the models are systematically over- or under-predicting energy and enstrophy at specific scales, which is critical for understanding their physical fidelity. The results of this analysis are presented in Section \ref{se:spectraresults}. The spectral error ratio values are visualized in log-log plots, which facilitate comparison to the characteristic power-law slopes of the kinetic energy and enstrophy spectra in turbulent flows. Deviations from the expected spectral slopes can indicate specific deficiencies in the model's ability to capture certain physical processes, such as energy cascade dynamics or small-scale dissipation.
