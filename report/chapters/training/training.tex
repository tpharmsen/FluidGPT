\section{Training procedure}

Training Framework and System Abstraction

To support large-scale experimentation across heterogeneous compute environments, we developed a custom training framework designed to seamlessly operate on both local devices and high-performance computing (HPC) systems. In particular, the framework targets SLURM-managed, container-based clusters while retaining full compatibility with single-node and multi-GPU setups. This abstraction enables identical training and validation logic to be reused across environments, ensuring reproducibility and minimizing environment-specific code paths.

The framework is fully modular and configurable through declarative configuration files, allowing model architectures, optimization parameters, data handling, and distributed training settings to be adjusted without modifying core training code. This design was critical for systematically comparing multiple model classes under controlled experimental conditions.

Models

Two foundation models were trained within this unified framework. The first is a deterministic autoregressive (AR) model, which predicts the next temporal state conditioned on a fixed history window. The second is a probabilistic flow-matching model, which learns a conditional distribution over future states and enables stochastic sampling during inference. For the flow-matching model, the prior-distribution is generated on the go with correlated gaussian noise. The noise is generated as pure gaussian noise, with a gaussian filter with a standard deviation of 4 in spatial directions and 1 in temporal direction applied to introduce correlation. Applying spatial-correlation of the prior distribution is not only reducing the sharpness of interfaces in the fluids, but also reduces the state-space from which the model has to learn to transform to the target distribution, making the learning task easier.

Distributed Training and Effective Batch Size

Training was performed using data parallelism across 8 devices, resulting in an effective batch size equal to eight times the per-device batch size of 176. This parallelism was essential to stabilize optimization and maintain throughput given the high-dimensional spatial-temporal data and large model capacity. Gradient synchronization was performed at each training step to ensure consistent updates across devices.

Precision Strategy

To balance numerical stability with computational efficiency, reduced-precision training using bfloat16 was employed for all forward and backward passes during training. Validation and evaluation were conducted in float32 precision to ensure accurate error metrics and fair model comparison. This mixed-precision strategy significantly reduced memory usage and improved throughput without observable degradation in training stability.

Optimization and Learning Rate Scheduling

Both models were trained using the same optimization strategy to ensure a fair comparison. Learning rates were tuned using a schedule consisting of an initial warmup phase, followed by stepwise decay at predefined milestones. The warmup phase mitigates optimization instability during early training, particularly for large models and distributed settings, while milestone-based decay improves convergence during later stages of training. Due to the sampling nature of the flow-matching model, a slightly higher initial learning rate was used compared to the deterministic AR model. Also, the flow-matching model is trained for longer at pre-decay learning rates to account for the increased complexity of learning a conditional distribution.

Hardware Platform

All large-scale experiments were conducted on the state of the art NVIDIA DGX B200 unified AI platform, leveraging its high-bandwidth interconnect, large GPU memory capacity and high compute power. Training and evaluation were executed within containerized environments, ensuring consistent software dependencies across runs.

For each dataset, 20\% of the available trajectories were reserved for validation and were not used during training. All validation metrics were computed exclusively on this held-out split. Validation was designed to assess both short-term predictive accuracy and long-horizon temporal stability of the learned dynamics.

\subsection{Model-Specific Validation}

\paragraph{Deterministic Autoregressive Model.}
The deterministic autoregressive (AR) model produces a static prediction for a given input sequence. Both single-step and rollout validations therefore yield a deterministic predicted trajectory, which is directly compared against ground truth using pointwise and spectral physics metrics.

\paragraph{Probabilistic Flow-Matching Model.}
The probabilistic flow-matching model defines a conditional distribution over future states. During validation, 16 independent samples were drawn for each input sequence. Metrics were computed over the resulting ensemble, enabling analysis of both the mean prediction and the variability across samples. 

\subsection{Velocity Integration for Flow Matching}
The flow-matching model predicts \emph{velocity increments} rather than absolute states. To generate the next physical state, a sample is first drawn from a prior distribution. The initial physical state is then integrated forward in \emph{flowtime} using the learned velocity field (the model), conditioned on the current physical state. Here, \emph{flowtime} $t$ is an auxiliary, continuous variable that parameterizes the transformation from the prior distribution to the predicted physical state. It is important to note that this is not the real physical time of the fluid system. Actually, it represents the trajectory along which the model deforms a sample into a physically plausible next state.

Formally, given an initial state $x_0$ and a learned velocity field $v_\theta(x,t)$, the integration in flowtime is expressed as
\begin{equation}
x(T) = x(0) + \int_{0}^{T} v_\theta\bigl(x(t), t\bigr) \, dt,
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the end of the flowtime interval. This continuous integration transforms the latent sample into a prediction in physical space. For the validation, we employed a simple Euler integration scheme with 20 discrete flowtime steps to approximate this integral.

\subsection{Single-Step Validation}
As an initial evaluation, both models were assessed using a single next-timeblock prediction setting. Given a ground-truth input sequence, the models predict the immediate next temporal block without feeding back their own predictions. This evaluation isolates the one-step transition accuracy and avoids error accumulation effects inherent to autoregressive rollouts. Thus this next-timeblock error metric consists of the average error of the next 5 timesteps.

\begin{equation}
\text{RRMSE} = \frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( y_i - \hat{y}_i \right)^2}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i^2}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\text{RAE} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left| y_i - \hat{y}_i \right|}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left| y_i \right|}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Autoregressive Rollout Validation}
To evaluate long-term behavior, both models were deployed in an autoregressive rollout configuration. Starting from an initial ground-truth context, model predictions were recursively fed back as inputs to generate future states. Prediction errors were computed at each timestep throughout the rollout horizon, enabling analysis of stability over time. 

\subsection{Spectral Diagnostics}
In addition to pointwise errors, validation focused on physically motivated spectral diagnostics. For each predicted and ground-truth state, the log-scaled kinetic energy spectrum $E(k)$ and enstrophy spectrum $Z(k)$ were computed by Fourier transforming the velocity field and aggregating contributions over isotropic shells in wavenumber space.

Scale-resolved spectral errors were defined as
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_E(k) = \left| \log E_{\mathrm{pred}}(k) - \log E_{\mathrm{true}}(k) \right|,
\qquad
\varepsilon_Z(k) = \left| \log Z_{\mathrm{pred}}(k) - \log Z_{\mathrm{true}}(k) \right|,
\end{equation}
allowing direct comparison of model fidelity across spatial scales.
