\chapter{Training and Validation}\label{ch:training}

This chapter details the training framework, hardware infrastructure, and validation methodology used to develop and evaluate the foundation models introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:models} that were trained on the unified collection of data described in Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets}. We describe the modular, multi-platform training system built to ensure reproducibility and fair comparison across model variants, along with the optimization strategy and compute resources used to train them at scale. We then present the validation protocol, encompassing pointwise error metrics, autoregressive rollout stability, and spectral diagnostics, designed to assess both predictive accuracy and physical fidelity of the resulting models.

\section{Challenges}

\subsection{Software}

As the training and validation of the foundation models is practically dependent on available compute resources, a modular framework needs to be developed to support preprocessing, training and validation of different datasets and models on various hardware platforms. This framework needs to supply consistent training behavior for all models to validate comparability between models. Furthermore, it needs to support parallel training on multiple GPUs, and needs to be able to run on different hardware platforms, including local workstations and high-performance computing clusters. Both SLURM-managed and dockerized container-based clusters need to be supported. 

\subsection{Hardware}

As the dataset size is large, and the models are complex, the task requires significant compute power. Specifically, the training phase of the models needs to be performed on a high-throughput multi-gpu compute cluster to ensure convergence of models within a reasonable amount of time/days. Also, the framework needs to have high interconnect bandwidth between GPUs and SSD memory to support quick data fetching, due to the inability to store the entire dataset in GPU memory. The hardware itself also needs to support mixed precision training to reduce memory usage and further improve training speed. 
Aside from high-throughput, the hardware needs to support high memory capacity to accommodate the large model sizes and batch sizes. Finally, SSD memory should be high enough to store the entire dataset, as well as the model checkpoints and logs, which is not trivial given the large size of the datasets.

\subsection{Validation metrics}

To compare the performance of different models, a set of relevant validation metrics needs to be defined. The metrics need to be able to capture the accuracy of raw predictions and for the probabilistic models, the quality of the predicted distribution. The metrics need to be able to capture the accuracy of short-term predictions and long-term rollouts, as well as the spectral fidelity of the predicted flow fields. 

\section{Training Procedure}

\subsection{Training Framework and System Abstraction}

To support large-scale experimentation across heterogeneous compute environments, a custom training framework was developed to operate seamlessly on both local workstations and high-performance computing (HPC) systems. The framework specifically targets SLURM-managed and container-based clusters while retaining full compatibility with single-node and multi-GPU setups. This abstraction ensures that identical training and validation logic is reused across environments, promoting reproducibility and eliminating environment-specific code paths. The framework is implemented in Python and leverages PyTorch Lightning for model definition, training, and evaluation.

The framework is fully modular and configured through declarative configuration files, enabling model architectures, optimization hyperparameters, data handling routines, and distributed training settings to be adjusted without modifying core training code. This design was critical for systematically comparing multiple model variations under controlled and reproducible experimental conditions.

\subsection{Model Training}

Four foundation models were trained within this unified framework. Descriptions and hyperparameters of the deterministic and probabilistic models are found in Chapter~\ref{ch:models}.

For the flow-matching model, the prior distribution is constructed on-the-fly using correlated Gaussian noise. Specifically, pure Gaussian noise is filtered with a Gaussian kernel with a standard deviation of 4 in the spatial directions and 1 in the temporal direction, introducing spatial and temporal correlation. This spatial correlation of the prior serves two purposes: it reduces the sharpness of fluid interfaces in the sampled prior, and it reduces the volume of the state space from which the model must learn to transport samples to the target distribution, thereby simplifying the learning task.

\subsection{Optimization and Learning Rate Scheduling}

Both model classes were trained using the a relatively similar Adam optimization strategy to facilitate a fair comparison. Learning rates were tuned via a schedule comprising an initial linear warmup phase followed by stepwise decay at predefined milestones. The warmup phase mitigates optimization instability during early training, which is particularly important for large models and distributed settings. Milestone-based decay subsequently improves convergence during the later stages of training.

Due to the increased size in learnable state space of the flow-matching approach, a slightly higher initial learning rate was applied to the flow-matching models compared to the deterministic AR counterparts. Additionally, flow-matching models were trained for a longer duration at initial learning rate to allow sufficient time for learning the more complex conditional distribution. All training was performed in \texttt{bfloat16} reduced precision to balance computational efficiency with numerical stability.

At each epoch the model is saved and the validation error is computed. The model with the lowest validation error is selected as the final model for full evaluation. This early stopping strategy ensures that the final reported results reflect the best generalization performance observed during training, rather than the final epoch which may be affected by overfitting.

\section{Hardware Platform and Parallelism}

All large-scale experiments were conducted on a brand new \textbf{NVIDIA DGX B200} unified AI platform \cite{nvidia2024dgxb200}, which provides a high-bandwidth GPU interconnect, large per-device memory capacity, and high aggregate compute throughput. To give the reader a sense of the amount of compute power, the NVIDIA DGX B200 is equipped with 8 NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs, yielding a possible throughput of 72 PetaFLOPS during training and 144 PetaFLOPS during inference. Note that one PetaFLOPS equals one quadrillion Floating Point Operations Per Second. The power of this platform lies especially in the high-bandwidth interconnect between GPUs, which allows for efficient data parallelism and model parallelism across multiple devices without incurring significant communication overhead. Using customized Dockerfiles, training and evaluation was executed within customized containerized environments to ensure consistent software dependencies across experimental runs, maximizing reproducibility and optimized device utilization. 

Training was distributed across 8 B200 GPUs using data parallelism, resulting in an effective batch size equal to eight times the per-device batch size of 176, yielding an effective batch size of 1408. This level of parallelism was essential both to stabilize the optimization dynamics and to maintain adequate throughput given the high-dimensional spatiotemporal data and large model capacity.

\section{Validation}

For each dataset, 20\% of the available trajectories were reserved as a held-out validation split and were not used during training. All validation metrics were computed exclusively on this split. Validation was designed to assess three different aspects of predictive quality short-term predictive accuracy, long-horizon temporal stability, and spectral fidelity to the underlying physics.

\subsection{Error Metrics}

The primary pointwise error metrics is defined as the per-sample relative absolute error, defined as

\begin{equation}
	\text{RAE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{ \left| y_i - \hat{y}_i \right|}{\left| y_i \right|},
\end{equation}

where $y_i$ is the ground-truth value, $\hat{y}_i$ is the corresponding prediction, and $N$ is the total number of samples. Naturally $i$ sums over all spatial and temporal dimensions of the predicted timeblock. These scale-invariant metrics allow meaningful comparison across datasets and model configurations. All validation was performed in \texttt{float32} precision to prioritize accurate error measurement over computational efficiency.

\subsection{Model- and Dataset-Specific Validation}

\paragraph{Deterministic Model.}
The deterministic AR model produces a single, fixed prediction for a given input sequence. Both single-step and rollout evaluations therefore yield a deterministic predicted trajectory, which is compared directly against the ground truth using pointwise and spectral metrics. Auto-regressive use of the deterministic approach simply means the predicted output timeblock is fed back into the input in iterative fashion.

\paragraph{Flow-Matching Model.}
The probabilistic flow-matching model defines a conditional distribution over future states. During validation, 16 independent samples were drawn for each input sequence, allowing analysis of the predicted distribution's spread. For the single next-timeblock validation on the \texttt{amira} dataset, a reduced number of 8 samples were drawn due to computational constraints of the dataset size. Error distributions are computed in a per-sample fashion and subsequently aggregated across all drawn samples.

As the flow-matching model predicts \emph{state increments} rather than absolute states, generating the next physical state requires numerical integration in \emph{flow-time} $t_f$. Flow-time is an auxiliary continuous variable that parameterizes the learned transport map from the prior distribution to the target distribution. Formally, given a prior sample $x(0)$ and a learned velocity field $v_\theta(x, t_f)$ conditioned on the current physical state, the predicted state is obtained as

\begin{equation}
	x(1) = x(0) + \int_{0}^{1} v_\theta\bigl(x(t_f), t_f\bigr) \, \mathrm{d}{t_f}.
\end{equation}

For validation, this integral is approximated using a simple Euler scheme with 20 discrete flow-time steps. The prior distribution used at inference time is identical to that used during training. After a sample has been mapped to a physical state through the integration process, it can be fed back as conditioning input for a new prediction. This conditioning combined with a new sample of the prior distribution is then passed to the model in order to enable autoregressive rollouts. Note that the flow-matching model's autoregressive rollout is inherently stochastic due to the sampling of the prior at each step, resulting in a distribution of predicted trajectories rather than a single deterministic trajectory. 

\subsubsection{Single-Step Validation}

As an initial evaluation, all models were assessed in a single next-timeblock prediction setting. Given a ground-truth input sequence of 5 timesteps, each model predicts the immediately following temporal block without feeding back its own predictions. This isolates single-step transition accuracy and eliminates error accumulation effects that arise in autoregressive rollouts. The reported metric is the average per-sample error over the next 5 predicted timesteps, aggregated across all input-output pairs in the validation dataset. Note that the models natively predict a timeblock of multiple timesteps at once, instead of a single timestep.

\subsubsection{Rollout Validation}

To evaluate long-term temporal behavior, all models were deployed in an autoregressive rollout configuration. Starting from the first 5 ground-truth physical states of each validation trajectory, model predictions were recursively fed back as inputs to generate the full future trajectory. Prediction errors were computed at each timestep throughout the rollout horizon, enabling analysis of error accumulation and long-term stability. Note that the first timestep containing the non-natural initialization conditions of the fluid trajectory is also included and thus this evaluation step assesses the model's ability to handle out-of-distribution flow states, which is a critical aspect of model robustness.

\subsubsection{Spectral Diagnostics}

In addition to pointwise metrics, validation included physics-informed spectral diagnostics to assess whether the models correctly reproduce the scale distribution of energy and enstrophy. For each predicted and ground-truth state, the log-scaled kinetic energy spectrum $E(k)$ and enstrophy spectrum $Z(k)$ were computed by Fourier-transforming the velocity field and aggregating spectral contributions over isotropic shells in wavenumber space, as described in Section \ref{sec:energyspectra}.

Scale-resolved spectral errors were defined as

\begin{equation}
	\bar{R}_E(k)
	=
	\frac{1}{N}
	\sum_{i=1}^{N}
	\frac{E_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(i)}(k)}
		{E_{\mathrm{true}}^{(i)}(k)},
	\qquad
	\bar{R}_Z(k)
	=
	\frac{1}{N}
	\sum_{i=1}^{N}
	\frac{Z_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(i)}(k)}
		{Z_{\mathrm{true}}^{(i)}(k)}.
\end{equation}

As each fluid trajectory has its own ground-truth reference spectra corresponding to the system state at that time $t$, it is not meaningful to show an average graph of the spectra across the dataset. Instead, we analyze the distribution of spectral errors across the dataset by calculating the ratio between the predicted and true spectra for each trajectory, and then average this ratio across all trajectories to obtain a distribution of spectral errors as a function of wavenumber. This approach allows us to assess whether the models are systematically over- or under-predicting energy and enstrophy at specific scales, which is critical for understanding their physical fidelity. Especially for handling turbulent flows, a single pointwise error metric is incapable of capturing whether the diverging predictions reproduce the underlying physics of the system. The spectral error ratio provides a scale-resolved view of model performance, revealing whether the models correctly capture the energy cascade and dissipation mechanisms that govern turbulent dynamics. 
\cite{kochkov2021machine}


The results of this analysis are presented in Section~\ref{se:spectraresults}. The spectral error ratio values are visualized in log-log plots, which facilitate comparison to the characteristic power-law slopes of the kinetic energy and enstrophy spectra in turbulent flows. Deviations from the expected spectral slopes can indicate specific deficiencies in the model's ability to capture certain physical processes, such as energy cascade dynamics or small-scale dissipation.
