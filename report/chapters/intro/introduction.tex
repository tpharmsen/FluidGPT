\chapter*{Introduction}

Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is the indispensable tool for designing and optimizing systems across virtually every engineering discipline, from aerospace and turbomachinery to climate modeling and biomedical devices. However, high-fidelity CFD simulations of complex, turbulent, or multi-physics systems remain computationally prohibitive, often requiring days or weeks of processing time. Recent years have shown that computational cost can be greatly reduced with the use of AI surrogate models. These models learn the underlying PDE behaviour directly from simulation data, promising to reduce prediction time from hours to milliseconds. Specifically, so called 'foundation' models can learn the dynamics of multiple PDE systems bundled in a single model. 

\section{Foundational model goal}
 
The creation of an effective AI surrogate model presents a critical challenge: achieving generalization across the vast parameter space of PDEs. Traditional models are often trained and locked to a single set of simulation parameters for multiple PDE systems. Our work tackles this limitation by including a wide domain of simulation parameters for a single PDE system; Navier Stokes. The primary goal is to design a single, unified AI architecture capable of accurately predicting future states across a wide variety of Navier-Stokes systems, including compressible and incompressible flows, diverse geometric constraints, and varying simulation settings from multi-source large-scale pretraining data.

\section{Dual approach to temporal stability}

While foundational models are often designed for accuracy of spatial structures, the accuracy of temporal progression of generated system states dictate its usability in the real world. To account for this gap in literature, this research focuses on stable time-progression of system states.
The approach to stability is two-fold;

\textbf{Short-Term Deterministic Prediction (Auto-regressive):} This scheme utilizes an Auto-regressive (AR) approach, predicting a few (bundled) time-steps into the future. This method is computationally straightforward and highly accurate for very short horizons. However, when deployed for long-sequence generation, this system is inherently susceptible to compounding errors: the small error in one predicted state is fed directly as input to predict the next, leading to an accumulation of error and divergence from the true physical trajectory.
  

\textbf{Long-Sequence Probabilistic Generation (Flow Matching):} This method shifts the focus from a short deterministic future to modeling the complex transition probability distribution over longer time steps. This shift is motivated by the physical nature of turbulent flows; the Navier-Stokes system is naturally diverging (sensitive to initial conditions), meaning small variations can lead to vastly different system states over time. Specifically, the breaking of the same high-energy vortex can result in vastly different small-energy vortex distributions. As Flow Matching is able to learn probability density distributions rather than single input-output pairs, this approach is naturally able to account for branches in the state space.

A significant gap exists in current literature, where reported results often focus on single-step accuracy rather than long-term temporal stability and physical consistency over extended rollouts. Yet, for any real-world application—be it aerodynamic design, weather forecasting, or control—the ability of the surrogate to remain stable and accurate over hundreds or thousands of steps is fundamental. By comparing these two methods, we directly address this critical stability and fidelity challenge.

\section*{Background and Related Work}

In this section, we place our work within the broader literature on neural surrogates for PDEs (especially Navier–Stokes), and we highlight both deterministic (e.g. convolutional / operator-based) and probabilistic / generative approaches, as well as more recent foundational model or multi-physics perspectives.

\paragraph{Neural Surrogate Models for Fluid Dynamics}
Early neural-network approaches to fluid simulation often used convolutional architectures operating on grid-based velocity/pressure fields to predict next-step increments or to emulate low-dimensional flow maps. For instance, \textit{Deep Fluids} \cite{kim2018deepfluids} uses a convolutional generative model over velocity fields, enforcing divergence-free velocity via architectural constraints, and evolves in latent space to accelerate simulation by orders of magnitude.

More generally, convolutional U-Net style architectures (or hybrid CNN + residual blocks) have been used to directly predict flow updates or corrections, sometimes augmented with physics-based regularization based on divergence.

On the other side, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) and physics-constrained deep surrogates attempt to encode the governing PDE residuals directly into the loss, thereby reducing reliance on large amounts of simulation data. Zhu et al. \cite{zhu2019physics} propose a physics-constrained deep learning framework for high-dimensional PDEs, incorporating the residual of the differential operator into the loss function. Similarly, Sun et al. \cite{sun2020surrogate} explore physics-constrained surrogates for fluid flows without fully relying on simulation data. Geometry-aware extensions, such as GAPINN \cite{haghighat2022gapinn}, allow handling non-trivial geometries by encoding latent representations of boundary shapes combined with spatial inputs.

While these methods have shown promise, they often struggle to scale to turbulent flows, to large Reynolds numbers, or to long temporal rollouts, due to accumulated error in auto-regressive modelling, lack of generalization, or numerical instability.

\paragraph{Operator Learning and Neural PDE / Neural-Operator Methods}
More recently, neural operator architectures (e.g. Fourier Neural Operator, DeepONet, Graph Neural Operator) have emerged to learn mappings between continuous function spaces rather than discrete pointwise mappings. These models tend to generalize across input parameter variations like boundary conditions, forcing fields, and discretizations of the geometry space, and have been applied successfully to a wide variety of PDEs \cite{li2020fourier, lu2021deeponet, li2020graph}. Many surrogate models in scientific machine learning adopt operator paradigms for scalability and generalization.

The notion of a foundation model for PDEs builds on the analogy to large-scale pretraining in language or vision: the model is pretrained on a diverse collection of PDE systems (or parameter settings) and then fine-tuned (or evaluated) on downstream tasks. For instance, MPP, POSEIDON, PROSE-FD, and DPOT aim to capture multi-physics systems in a unified neural architecture \cite{polymathicAI2024}. Another example is P3D \cite{yang2025p3d}, which introduces a hybrid CNN–Transformer backbone to train deterministic and probabilistic surrogates over multiple PDEs (14 types) in 3D, enabling scaling to high-resolution turbulent flows and demonstrating stable autoregressive rollout performance.

These PDE foundation models underscore an important trend: moving from case-by-case surrogate training to broadly pretrained models with generalization across parameter domains. Yet, most such works still focus on short-horizon accuracy or per-step error rather than explicit long-term consistency or statistical fidelity.

\paragraph{Probabilistic / Generative Modeling of Fluid Flows}
A critical challenge for long-sequence prediction in chaotic or turbulent systems is that deterministic rollouts will diverge due to error accumulation. Probabilistic or stochastic surrogate models attempt to capture not just the mean trajectory, but the distribution of plausible futures, or at least embed uncertainty quantification (UQ) in predictions.

One of the earlier works is the \textit{Probabilistic Neural Network for Fluid Flow Surrogate Modeling} by Maulik et al. \cite{maulik2020probabilistic}, which treats the target variables as conditionally Gaussian and predicts both mean and variance to yield confidence intervals on predictions. This probabilistic surrogate was evaluated on canonical fluid dynamical regression tasks (cylinder wake, shallow water, etc.). In turbulence modeling, \textit{Uncertainty-Aware Surrogate Models for Airfoil Flow Simulations} \cite{zheng2023uncertainty} propose the use of denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) to approximate the full posterior distribution over velocity fields, demonstrating that DDPMs can generate diverse, physically plausible samples with uncertainty estimates. They also compare flow matching variants to alleviate slow sampling in diffusion.

More generally, Gao et al. \cite{gao2024bayesian} frame Bayesian conditional diffusion models for spatiotemporal turbulence, yielding a probabilistic generation framework for turbulence fields. Mücke and Sanderse \cite{mucke2025physicsaware} propose physics-aware generative models for turbulent fluid flows based on stochastic interpolants, embedding energy consistency and divergence constraints within generative modeling to preserve physical invariants over long rollouts.

In multi-fidelity settings, Geneva and Zabaras \cite{geneva2020multi} use a conditional invertible neural network (normalizing flows) plus recurrent LSTM connections to generate high-fidelity turbulent fields conditioned on inexpensive low-fidelity inputs, allowing fast sampling of stochastic corrections. Finally, in the broader PDE domain, Vadeboncoeur et al. \cite{vadeboncoeur2022probabilistic} introduce a physics-driven latent variable model that simultaneously models forward and inverse maps, with uncertainty quantification via variational inference and PDE residual regularization.

These works illustrate a trend: deterministic surrogate modeling → uncertainty-aware models → full generative probabilistic modeling over solution fields.

\paragraph{Gaps, Challenges, and Our Positioning}
From this survey, several gaps remain, which our work directly addresses:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Long-Term Stability and Physical Consistency:} Many existing studies report per-step or short-horizon error metrics but do not extensively validate long rollouts in turbulent regimes. Accumulation of error and divergence from physical invariants (e.g. energy, enstrophy, divergence-free constraint) remain major obstacles.
	\item \textbf{Unified Modeling Across Parameter Regimes:} While foundation model approaches exist, they rarely confront the full diversity within a single system (e.g. compressible vs. incompressible Navier–Stokes, varying Mach/Reynolds regimes, geometric constraints) or switch between deterministic and probabilistic modes.
	\item \textbf{Efficient Probabilistic Generation:} Diffusion-based generative models tend to be slow in inference; flow matching, stochastic interpolants, or other faster generative techniques need more exploration in PDE contexts.
	\item \textbf{Architectural Trade-offs and Inference Efficiency:} Larger transformer-based PDE models are powerful but heavy; lighter architectures (e.g. U-Net-based foundation models) show promise but require careful design to maintain generalization.
\end{itemize}

In this work, by comparing an auto-regressive deterministic surrogate and a probabilistic generative model (via flow matching) within a shared foundational framework, we aim to illuminate the trade-offs between accuracy, rollout stability, and efficiency. Moreover, by training across a wide parameter regime of Navier–Stokes flows and emphasizing temporal stability beyond single-step metrics, we contribute a more rigorous evaluation of surrogate modeling for real-world CFD applications.
