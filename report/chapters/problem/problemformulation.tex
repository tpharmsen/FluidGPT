\section{Navier-Stokes System}


\noindent
The Navier–Stokes equations describe the dynamics of fluid flow through a set of coupled, nonlinear partial differential equations that enforce conservation of both mass and momentum. In this work, we focus on the two-dimensional, isothermal case, governed by the following equations:

\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u}) = 0,
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{u} = -\frac{1}{\rho} \nabla p + \frac{1}{\rho} \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\tau} + \mathbf{f},
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	B_i(\mathbf{u}, p)|_{\partial \Omega} = g_i, \quad \mathbf{u}(t=0) = A, \quad x \in \Omega = [0,1]^2.
\end{equation}

\noindent
Here, $\rho$ denotes density, $\mathbf{u}$ the velocity vector field, $p$ the pressure, $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ the viscous stress tensor, and $\mathbf{f}$ an external forcing term. The first equation expresses conservation of mass (continuity), while the second represents momentum conservation. Boundary and initial conditions are specified by $B_i$ and $A$, respectively.
In the special case of incompressible flow, the simplification $\frac{d\rho}{dt} = 0$ is applied, which reduces the complexity of the operator’s input–output mapping.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent
\textbf{Characteristics of the Navier–Stokes System.}
The Navier–Stokes system shows complex dynamical behavior characterized by creation, advection, and dissipation of vortical structures, often referred to as eddies. Large eddies break down into smaller eddies in a cascading process, transferring energy across lowering scales until it dissipates at the smallest (viscous) level. This process is chaotic and highly sensitive to initial conditions, leading to stochastic-like macroscopic behavior even though the governing equations are deterministic. Capturing this behavior accurately is a core challenge for surrogate models, especially those that must generalize across varying flow regimes and boundary conditions.

\section{Problem Definition}

The objective of this work is to construct a model $\mathcal{M}$ that predicts the future evolution of a continuous-time, partial differential equation (PDE) system, specifically the \textbf{Navier-Stokes equations}, across a wide and diverse parameter space.

The system state is defined by the flow variable $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t)$, which is the 2D \textbf{velocity field}:
$$
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t) = [u_x(\mathbf{x}, t), u_y(\mathbf{x}, t)]^T,
$$
where $u_x$ and $u_y$ are the velocity components in the $x$ and $y$ directions, respectively, in a spatial domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ at time $t$. The governing physics are implicitly defined by the Navier-Stokes equations, which can be summarized in the general form:

\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u}, \nabla \mathbf{u}, \nabla^2 \mathbf{u}; \mathbf{c}) = 0 \quad \text{in } \Omega \times (0, \infty)
\end{equation}

with appropriate initial conditions and boundary conditions. The vector $\mathbf{c} \in \mathcal{C}$ denotes a set of \textbf{system parameters} (e.g., Reynolds number $Re$, domain geometry, and boundary conditions) that condition the flow.

\subsection{The Prediction Task}

In the context of a discrete-time, data-driven surrogate model, the spatial domain $\Omega$ is discretized into a grid, $\mathbf{x} \in \Omega_h$ with resolution $128^2$. The system state at a time step $t_i$ is represented by a high-dimensional tensor $\mathbf{U}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{N_x \times N_y \times 2}$, where $N_x, N_y$ are the number of grid points in the spatial dimensions, and $2$ represents the input flow variables ($u_x$ and $u_y$).

Given an input sequence of $T_0 > 1$ historical flow states:
$$
\mathbf{S}_{\text{in}} = \{ \mathbf{U}_0, \mathbf{U}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{U}_{T_0-1} \},
$$
the core prediction problem is to model the evolution operator $\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{c}}$ such that the model $\mathcal{M}$ accurately predicts the subsequent $T$ future states:
$$
\mathbf{S}_{\text{out}} = \{ \mathbf{U}_{T_0}, \mathbf{U}_{T_0+1}, \ldots, \mathbf{U}_{T_0+T-1} \}.
$$
The model $\mathcal{M}$ is a function of the input sequence $\mathbf{S}_{\text{in}}$ and the system parameter vector $\mathbf{c}$.
$$
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{S}_{\text{in}}) \approx \mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{S}_{\text{in}})
$$

\subsection{Dual Modelling Approach}

This problem is tackled using two distinct modeling approaches, which define the specific mathematical targets of the prediction. Both models now condition their output on a sequence of the $k$ most recent past states, $\mathbf{S}_{\text{hist}} = \{ \mathbf{U}_{i-k+1}, \ldots, \mathbf{U}_i \}$, and are trained to predict a bundle of $\Delta T$ future states, $\mathbf{S}_{\text{pred}} = \{ \mathbf{U}_{i+1}, \ldots, \mathbf{U}_{i+\Delta T} \}$.

\paragraph{1. Short-Sequence Deterministic Prediction (Auto-Regressive, $\Delta T = 3$)}
This approach models the conditional expectation of a short-sequence bundle of future states, $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{S}_{\text{out}} | \mathbf{S}_{\text{in}}]$. It utilizes a function $f_{\theta}$ that maps the historical sequence to the predicted future sequence:
$$
f_{\theta}: \mathbf{S}_{\text{in}} \mapsto \mathbf{S}_{\text{out}}
$$
The predicted sequence of states is:
$$
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\text{out}} = f_{\theta}(\mathbf{S}_{\text{in}}).
$$
For long rollouts, the prediction system is \textbf{auto-regressive}: the predicted sequence $\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{\text{pred}}$ is sequentially shifted and fed back as the new historical input $\mathbf{S}_{\text{hist}}$ to predict the next $\Delta T$ steps.

The learning objective for the model parameters $\theta$ is the minimization of a mean-squared error (MSE) loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{AR}}$, measuring the error over the entire predicted sequence bundle:
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AR}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{S}_{\text{in}}, \mathbf{S}_{\text{out}}) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \| \mathbf{S}_{\text{out}} - f_{\theta}(\mathbf{S}_{\text{in}}) \|_2^2 \right].
$$
This method prioritizes short-term accuracy and computation time above long-term accuracy.

\paragraph{2. Long-Sequence Probabilistic Prediction (Flow Matching, $\Delta T = 12$)}
Given the turbulent nature of the system, the evolution over a long horizon $T$ is better described by the transition of a probability distribution $p(\mathbf{U}_{T_0+T} | \mathbf{S}_{\text{in}})$ rather than a deterministic one. % This model uses the full input history $\mathbf{S}_{\text{in}}$ (where $T_0$ is the total history length) to model the distribution of the final state $\mathbf{U}_{T_0+T}$.

The goal is to train a model $\mathcal{M}_{\phi}$ with parameters $\phi$ that effectively models this conditional probability distribution:
$$
\mathcal{M}_{\phi}(\mathbf{S}_{\text{in}}) \approx p(\mathbf{U}_{T_0+T} | \mathbf{S}_{\text{in}}).
$$
\textbf{Flow Matching} achieves this by learning the \textbf{velocity field} $\mathbf{v}_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}, \tau)$ that transforms a simple prior distribution (like Gaussian noise) into the complex target distribution $p$ via a continuous-time ordinary differential equation (ODE) trajectory: $\frac{d\mathbf{z}}{d\tau} = \mathbf{v}_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}, \tau)$, where $\tau \in [0, 1]$ is an artificial time. By conditioning on the previous system states $\mathbf{S}_{\text{in}}$, the model can better identify the appropriate statistical flow regime, allowing it to efficiently sample from the manifold of physically plausible solutions and ensuring long-term temporal stability and statistical fidelity. Note that the velocity field and artificial time in this context are different definitions than the velocity field and time of the physical Navier-Stokes system.

\section{Governing System and Data Scope}

The core physical system addressed by this research is flow governed by the Navier–Stokes equations. The problem formulation must account for the complexity and variability inherent in large-scale pretraining data sources, which necessitates generalization across diverse flow regimes and boundary conditions.

\subsection{Governing Partial Differential Equations (PDEs)}

The foundational model is tasked with learning the dynamics of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, defined by the conservation of momentum and mass. Given a spatial domain $\Omega$ and time $t \in [0, T]$, the system state $\mathbf{u}(x, t) = (u_x, u_y, P)$ (velocity components and pressure) is governed by:

\begin{align*}
	\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} &= - \frac{1}{\rho} \nabla P + \nu \nabla^2 \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{f} \quad & \text{(Momentum)} \\
	\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} &= 0 \quad & \text{(Continuity)}
\end{align*}

where $\rho$ is the fluid density, $P$ is the pressure field, $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity (inversely related to the Reynolds number $Re$), and $\mathbf{f}$ represents external body forces.

\subsection{Generalization Space and Data Abstraction}

The primary objective is to develop a model $F$ capable of generalization across a wide domain of system parameters $\mathbf{P}$, including:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Geometric Constraints:} Varying obstacle shapes (e.g., airfoils, cylinders) and domain boundaries.
	\item \textbf{Inflow Conditions ($u_\text{in}$):} Diverse velocities and boundary layer specifications.
	\item \textbf{Viscosity ($\nu$):} Parameterized flow regimes spanning laminar ($Re \sim 10^2$) to highly turbulent ($Re \sim 10^5$).
\end{enumerate}

The model is formulated as a mapping
\[
F : \mathbf{U}_t, \mathbf{P} \mapsto \mathbf{U}_{t+\Delta t},
\]
where $\mathbf{U}$ is the discrete, gridded state tensor of $(\mathbf{u}, P)$ and $\mathbf{P}$ is an embedding of the non-spatial system parameters.

\section{The Temporal Stability Problem}

The fundamental problem addressed is the inherent instability of conventional deterministic (auto-regressive) AI surrogates in simulating chaotic, high–Reynolds number flows over extended time horizons.

\subsection{Computational Objective: Minimizing Cumulative Error}

Given an arbitrary initial state $\mathbf{U}_0$ and system parameters $\mathbf{P}$, the prediction task is to generate a sequence of states $\hat{\mathbf{U}}^1, \hat{\mathbf{U}}^2, \dots, \hat{\mathbf{U}}^N$ that accurately approximates the ground truth states $\mathbf{U}^1, \mathbf{U}^2, \dots, \mathbf{U}^N$.

The deterministic autoregressive approach minimizes the $\ell_2$ loss at each step:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AR}} = \sum_{t=0}^{N-1} \left\| F_{\theta}(\mathbf{U}_t, \mathbf{P}) - \mathbf{U}_{t+\Delta t} \right\|_2^2.
\]
However, in actual deployment, the surrogate is recursively applied:
\[
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t+1} = F_{\theta}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_t, \mathbf{P}).
\]
The problem of \textbf{cumulative error propagation} is defined as the rapid divergence of the rollout error $E_{\text{rollout}}(N)$ as $N$ increases:
\[
E_{\text{rollout}}(N) = \left\| \hat{\mathbf{U}}_N - \mathbf{U}_N \right\|_2.
\]
This divergence renders the surrogate unusable for real-world applications requiring long time rollouts, which is the central limitation this research aims to overcome.

\subsection{Probabilistic Formulation for Stability}

To mitigate the cumulative error problem, the second approach formulates the prediction as modeling the transition probability distribution $p(\mathbf{U}_{t+\Delta t} \mid \mathbf{U}_t, \mathbf{P})$. This recognizes the chaotic nature of Navier–Stokes dynamics, where multiple physical outcomes $\mathbf{U}_{t+\Delta t}$ are valid given $\mathbf{U}_t$.

The Flow Matching objective is to learn a noise-conditioned score function $s_\phi$ that transforms a simple prior distribution into the complex target distribution $p(\mathbf{U}_{t+\Delta t} \mid \mathbf{U}_t, \mathbf{P})$:

\[
\min_{\phi} \, \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{U}_t, \mathbf{P}, t, \mathbf{x}} 
\left[ 
\left\| 
s_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}, t \mid \mathbf{U}_t, \mathbf{P}) 
- \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p(\mathbf{x}, t \mid \mathbf{U}_t, \mathbf{P}) 
\right\|_2^2 
\right].
\]

This shifts the metric of success from a single deterministic prediction to the fidelity of the generated ensemble statistics, ensuring \textbf{physical consistency} rather than point-to-point accuracy over long time horizons.

\section{Definition of Success Metrics}

To rigorously compare the AR and Flow Matching approaches, success will be evaluated across three critical dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Short-Term Fidelity ($\text{Fidelity}_1$):} The step-wise $\ell_2$ error (Equation 1) for the first $K = 5$ predictions. This measures baseline predictive capacity.
	\item \textbf{Long-Term Temporal Stability ($\text{Stability}_N$):} The maximum rollout horizon $N$ (e.g., $N > 100$ steps) before the $\ell_2$ rollout error (Equation 2) exceeds a defined tolerance threshold $\epsilon$.
	\item \textbf{Physical Consistency ($\text{Consistency}_{KE}$):} The preservation of key physical quantities over the full rollout $N$. Specifically, the error in the kinetic energy spectrum, e.g., the cumulative error in the time-average of kinetic energy
	\[
	\sum_{t=0}^{N} \mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\rho \|\mathbf{u}_t\|_2^2\right].
	\]
\end{enumerate}
