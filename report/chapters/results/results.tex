\section{Single-step prediction}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figs/results/ssbox_rae.png}
	\caption{Boxplot of the relative absolute error of the single next-timeblock prediction for each of the four surrogate foundation models, grouped by dataset. The models are trained on the same trajectory data split and evaluated in zero-shot fashion on the validation dataset. For the flow matching models, FM-d2 and FM-d3, 16 samples are taken for each input-output pair. The flow matching model FM-d2 mostly outperforms the other models, except on the pdebench-incomp and pdegym-sines datasets. All models struggle to predict the more complex pdebench-comp dataset.}
	\label{fig:ssbox}
\end{figure}

For every validation dataset, the next-timeblock prediction error is computed for each model and visualised in Figure~\ref{fig:ssbox} in the form of a boxplot. The average per-sample relative absolute error is calculated for the full predicted timeblock.

\paragraph{Probabilistic vs.\ deterministic models.} The flow matching model \texttt{FM-d2} outperforms the deterministic counterparts on nearly all datasets. The deterministic models are constrained to produce a single point estimate of the subsequent system states, whereas a probabilistic model learns a distribution over plausible futures and can better capture the sensitive or multi-modal nature of the solution space. By conditioning on a learned prior and flowing towards the target distribution, the flow matching models are better equipped to represent uncertainty in the predicted state block.

\paragraph{Effect of dataset complexity.} Error magnitudes are notably higher on higher-dimensional datasets characterised by strong turbulence or compressible flow conditions, compared to datasets satisfying the incompressibility assumption. The compressible and highly turbulent regimes exhibit sharper gradients and finer-scale structure that are more challenging to predict across multiple time steps simultaneously.

\paragraph{Exception: PDEGym sines dataset.} The probabilistic models do not demonstrate a clear advantage on the PDEGym sines dataset. We attribute this to a possible mismatch between the dominant frequencies present in the data and the frequency initialisation of the prior distribution used in the flow matching procedure. This frequency mismatch, combined with the model's inability to preserve sharp interfaces in the fluid, makes the transport problem more difficult, yielding a less accurate output overall.

\paragraph{Scaling behaviour.} A further distinction between the two model families emerges when comparing the \texttt{d2} and \texttt{d3} variants within each. Changing model depth and parameter count yields a more pronounced reduction in error for the probabilistic models than for the deterministic ones. This suggests that the additional capacity is more effectively utilised when the model has the expressive power to refine a distributional estimate, whereas deterministic models may saturate earlier or be limited by the intrinsic irreducibility of the static point-estimation objective.

\section{Rollout prediction}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figs/results/msgraph_all_rae.png}
	\caption{3x3 subplot of the relative absolute error of rollout predictions for all models, grouped per dataset. The models are used in autoregressive fashion to predict the full-length trajectory, starting with the first available input timeblock containing the initialization conditions. The flow matching models take 16 global rollout samples for each trajectory in the zero-shot validation dataset, drawing a new prior distribution sample for each individual input-output prediction during the autoregressive rollout. The flow matching model \texttt{FM-d2} outperforms all other models except on \texttt{pdegym-sines}. Error accumulation patterns differ across datasets and models.}
	\label{fig:rollout}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:rollout} presents the autoregressive rollout error across all nine datasets, where each model is applied recurrently to predict successive blocks of system states. The rollout length varies between datasets, reflecting differences in the temporal extent of each benchmark.

\paragraph{Error accumulation rate.} The flow matching models accumulate error more slowly over the course of the rollout on the majority of datasets. Beyond the lower absolute error observed in the previous section, this suggests that the probabilistic models produce predictions that are more dynamically consistent, limiting the compounding of errors across autoregressive steps. On several datasets, the probabilistic models also yield less divergent rollout trajectories, indicating that the predicted system states remain closer to a physically plausible regime for longer.

\paragraph{Intra-family scaling behaviour.} A clear asymmetry emerges in how the two model families respond to increased capacity. The two deterministic models produce nearly identical rollout curves across datasets, suggesting that additional depth and parameters provide limited benefit within the deterministic framework and that these models may be approaching the ceiling of what point-estimation objectives can recover. In contrast, the two probabilistic variants differ substantially from one another: the deeper, higher-parameter flow matching model exhibits a marked reduction in error accumulation relative to its smaller counterpart, confirming the scaling trend observed in the single-step error analysis and indicating that additional capacity is more productively absorbed when learning a distributional representation of the dynamics.

\paragraph{Boundary artefacts in short-horizon datasets.} On datasets with shorter temporal block lengths, the rollout curves exhibit characteristic spikes at the edges of each predicted block. These artefacts are consistent with strong information spread at the temporal boundaries of the block prediction: the model has limited context near the edges of each window, producing elevated local error that then partially recovers toward the block interior. This effect is most pronounced for the deterministic models, suggesting that the probabilistic formulation provides some degree of regularisation at block boundaries, possibly through the smoothing effect of the learned prior.

\paragraph{Dataset-specific observations.} First, the PDEGym sines dataset again favours the deterministic models, consistent with the hypothesis raised previously that the prior frequency initialisation is misspecified for this benchmark. Second, the PDEBench incompressible dataset exhibits the highest error accumulation of all evaluated benchmarks across all models. The fluid is subject to a forcing term that drives the dynamics, although none of the models are provided with a symbolic forcing signal as input. The models must therefore account for the effect of an unobserved driver, making accurate rollout prediction substantially harder and introducing a persistent source of error that compounds with each autoregressive step.

\section{Spectrum analysis}\label{se:spectraresults}

Point-wise error metrics do not capture whether a model reproduces the correct \emph{distribution of energy across scales}, since two fields can have identical point-wise error yet very different spectral content. To assess spectral fidelity directly, the best-performing models, \texttt{FM-d2} and \texttt{AR-d2}, were selected for detailed spectral analysis. Figure~\ref{fig:energyspectrum} presents the scaled energy spectrum error ratio $\epsilon_E$ for three representative datasets: \textit{amira}, \textit{pdegym-sines}, and \textit{pdegym-bb}. The remaining datasets show similar trends and are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:spectr}. For each dataset, the energy spectrum is computed from both the predicted and reference trajectories at two rollout timesteps, following the incompressibility assumption, and the weighted ratio between the two is calculated per wavenumber as defined in Equation~\ref{eq:weighted_spectral_error}. For \texttt{FM-d2}, 16 rollout samples are drawn per trajectory; the median across samples is plotted together with the interquartile range (IQR) as a shaded band, giving a probabilistic spread that the single deterministic \texttt{AR-d2} rollout cannot provide. Values of $\epsilon_E$ above 1 indicate overprediction of energy at that wavenumber, values below 1 indicate underprediction.

\paragraph{Spectral ranges and overall fidelity.} Each spectrum can be read in terms of three characteristic ranges. The first is the large-scale, energy-containing range at low wavenumbers, dominated by the largest vortical structures. The second is the inertial range at intermediate wavenumbers, and the third is the dissipation range at high wavenumbers. Across all three ranges the underlying physics is well captured by both models, with only small deviations from the reference spectra, most notably in the larger structures. The flow matching model in particular performs strongly, both over short spatial scales and over long temporal horizons: its median and IQR sit consistently and significantly below the error levels of the deterministic \texttt{AR-d2} baseline, confirming that the probabilistic samples reproduce the reference energy distribution more faithfully than the deterministic prediction.

\paragraph{Exception: PDEGym sines dataset.} As with the single-step and rollout results, the \textit{pdegym-sines} dataset again stands out. Here the deterministic model attains errors similar in magnitude to those of the flow matching model, and even outperforms it over a specific wavenumber range, which explains why \texttt{AR-d2} performs comparatively better on this dataset overall.

\paragraph{Temporal degradation and dissipation-range scale dampening.} Some degradation in spectral fidelity is visible over longer rollout horizons, appearing as an order-of-magnitude shift in $\epsilon_E$ between the two evaluated timesteps for a given dataset. The error nonetheless remains bounded to at most $\mathcal{O}(10^{-2})$ across all datasets. Furthermore, the low error visible at the highest wavenumbers should be interpreted with some caution, as it is partly an artefact of the scaling used in $\epsilon_E$: since these wavenumbers contribute negligibly to the total spectral energy, the weighting effectively suppresses their influence on the plotted ratio, rather than reflecting genuinely accurate prediction in the dissipation range.

\paragraph{Enstrophy spectrum.} To assess whether this spectral fidelity extends to rotational structures, the enstrophy spectrum error ratio $\epsilon_Z$ is computed for the \textit{amira} dataset and shown in Figure~\ref{fig:enstrophyspectrum_amira}. The flow matching median and IQR lie significantly below the deterministic \texttt{AR-d2} curve at both timesteps. This indicates that the flow matching model not only reproduces the correct distribution of kinetic energy across scales, but also captures the vortical, rotational structure of the flow more accurately than its deterministic counterpart, reinforcing the overall picture of strong spectral fidelity for the probabilistic modelling approach. Similar trends occur across the enstrophy spectra of the remaining datasets, which can be viewed in Appendix~\ref{app:spectr}.
