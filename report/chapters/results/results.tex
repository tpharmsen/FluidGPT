Figure~\ref{fig:____________} reports the sum of squares error across all nine datasets, 
revealing several consistent trends in model performance.

\textbf{Probabilistic vs.\ deterministic models.} The best flow matching model outperforms the best deterministic counterparts on nearly all datasets. This is expected given the 
inherent stochasticity of turbulent fluid dynamics: a deterministic model is constrained 
to produce a single point estimate of the subsequent system state, whereas a probabilistic 
model learns a distribution over plausible futures and can better capture the multi-modal 
nature of the solution space. By conditioning on a learned prior and flowing towards the 
target distribution, the flow matching models are better equipped to represent uncertainty 
in the predicted state block. %The larger error bars of the probabilitic models on the compressible dataset (pdebench-comp) indicate a larger uncertainty within the diverging state-space of the navier-stokes time progression. 

\textbf{Effect of dataset complexity.} Error magnitudes are notably higher on 
higher-dimensional datasets characterised by strong turbulence or compressible flow 
conditions, compared to datasets satisfying the incompressibility assumption. This aligns 
with the increased complexity of the underlying dynamics: compressible and highly turbulent 
regimes exhibit sharper gradients and finer-scale structure that are more challenging to 
predict across multiple time steps simultaneously.

\textbf{Exception: PDEGym sines dataset.} The probabilistic models do not demonstrate 
a clear advantage on the PDEGym sines dataset. We attribute this to a mismatch between 
the dominant frequencies present in the data and the frequency initialisation of the prior 
distribution used in the flow matching procedure. Due to a bigger mismatch in frequencies and the inability of the model to create shard interfaces in the fluid, the transport problem becomes a more difficult task, yielding a less inaccurate output of the model.

\textbf{Scaling behaviour.} A further distinction between the two model families emerges 
when comparing shallow and deep variants within each. Changing model depth and parameter 
count yields a more pronounced reduction in error for the probabilistic models than for 
the deterministic ones. This suggests that the additional capacity is more effectively 
utilised when the model has the expressive power to refine a distributional estimate, 
whereas deterministic models may saturate earlier or be limited by the intrinsic 
irreducibility of the static point-estimation objective.

Figure~\ref{fig:____________} presents the autoregressive rollout MSE across all nine 
datasets, where each model is applied recurrently to predict successive blocks of system 
states. The rollout length varies between datasets, reflecting differences in the 
temporal extent of each benchmark.

\textbf{Error accumulation rate.} The flow matching models accumulate error more slowly 
over the course of the rollout on the majority of datasets. Beyond the lower absolute 
error observed in the previous section, this suggests that the probabilistic models 
produce predictions that are more dynamically consistent, limiting the compounding of 
errors across autoregressive steps. On several datasets, the probabilistic models also 
yield less divergent rollout trajectories, indicating that the predicted system states 
remain closer to a physically plausible regime for longer.

\textbf{Intra-family scaling behaviour.} A clear asymmetry emerges in how the two model 
families respond to increased capacity. The two deterministic models produce nearly 
identical rollout curves across datasets, suggesting that additional depth and parameters 
provide limited benefit within the deterministic framework and that these models may be 
approaching the ceiling of what point-estimation objectives can recover. In contrast, the 
two probabilistic variants differ substantially from one another: the deeper, 
higher-parameter flow matching model exhibits a marked reduction in error accumulation 
relative to its smaller counterpart, confirming the scaling trend observed in the SS 
error analysis and indicating that additional capacity is more productively absorbed when 
learning a distributional representation of the dynamics.

\textbf{Boundary artefacts in short-horizon datasets.} On datasets with shorter temporal 
block lengths, the rollout curves exhibit characteristic spikes at the edges of each 
predicted block. These artefacts are consistent with strong information spread at the 
temporal boundaries of the block prediction: the model has limited context near the edges 
of each window, producing elevated local error that then partially recovers toward the 
block interior. This effect is most pronounced for the deterministic models, suggesting 
that the probabilistic formulation provides some degree of regularisation at block 
boundaries, possibly through the smoothing effect of the learned prior.

\textbf{Dataset-specific observations.} Two datasets warrant individual discussion. First, 
the PDEGym sines dataset again favours the deterministic models, consistent with the 
hypothesis raised in Section that the prior frequency initialisation is 
misspecified for this benchmark. Second, the PDEBench incompressible dataset exhibits 
the highest error accumulation of all evaluated benchmarks across all models. This is 
attributable to the presence of external body forcing in this dataset: the fluid is 
subject to a forcing term that drives the dynamics, yet none of the models are provided 
with this forcing signal as input. The models must therefore account for the effect of 
an unobserved driver, making accurate rollout prediction substantially harder and 
introducing a persistent source of error that compounds with each autoregressive step.
